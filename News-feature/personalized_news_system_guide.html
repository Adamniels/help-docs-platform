01

What you are actually building

Generic news aggregators maximize engagement for a mass audience. What you need is different: a system that acts as a personal intelligence layer — one that understands your specific domains, curates with taste, and improves continuously from how you actually engage with it.

Core insight

The state of the art in 2025 is not a single algorithm. It is a pipeline of complementary layers: a fast content model to filter noise, a rich user model to represent your evolving interests, a ranking layer that reasons about relevance, and a feedback loop that keeps all three improving over time. Getting any one layer right is easy. Getting all four working together well is the actual hard problem.

The research literature distinguishes between three generations of approaches:

Three generations of personalized news

Gen 1

Keyword and category filtering

User subscribes to topics. System matches headlines. No learning. Still common in RSS readers.

Gen 2

Collaborative + content-based hybrid

Click history feeds an embedding model. Similar users suggest new content. Google News, MIND dataset era.

Gen 3

LLM-native user modeling

Language model reasons about WHY something is relevant to you. Explains its choices. Adapts to intent, not just history.

Gen 4

Agentic intelligence layer

Proactive retrieval. Connects news to your ongoing projects and goals. Emerging — where your platform should head.

02

The six major approaches

These are not mutually exclusive. Production systems at scale always combine several. The question is which ones to prioritize for your use case, and in what order to build them.

Content-based filtering with semantic embeddings

Foundational

Each article is embedded into a high-dimensional vector that captures its semantic meaning — not just its keywords. Your interest profile is also a vector, constructed from the articles you have read, saved, or spent time on. Relevance is computed as cosine similarity between the article vector and your profile vector.

This is the most important layer to get right first. It works from day one without any other users, handles totally novel topics well, and gives you full explainability. Modern embedding models like text-embedding-3-large or open-source alternatives like all-mpnet-base-v2 produce representations rich enough to capture nuanced domain interest.

The key improvement over simple keyword matching is that you can represent your interest in "autonomous systems safety" and it will surface articles about "drone regulation frameworks" that never use those words, because the embedding space understands the semantic relationship.

Vector DB (pgvector, Qdrant, Weaviate) Cosine similarity scoring User profile as average or weighted embedding

Hierarchical user interest modeling

Modern

Rather than a single user embedding, this approach represents your interests as a tree. The root captures your overall reading identity. The second level holds coarse domains like "AI research," "Swedish tech industry," "software architecture." The third level holds fine-grained sub-interests like "transformer interpretability" or "monorepo tooling."

Research from Microsoft (HieRec, 2021) showed that multi-granularity interest representation significantly outperforms flat single-vector approaches. The practical benefit for you: the system can surface both articles deeply relevant to a specific narrow interest and broader articles that connect across domains, because it is matching at different levels of your hierarchy simultaneously.

This also naturally solves the problem of diverse interests. You care about both distributed systems and AI safety, and those should not collapse into a blurry average — they should be maintained as distinct sub-trees.

Multi-vector user representation Topic taxonomy Attention pooling across sub-interests

LLM-powered relevance reasoning (RecPrompt / PBNR style)

Modern

Rather than just computing similarity scores, you prompt a language model with your interest profile and a candidate article and ask it to reason about relevance. This approach, formalized in papers like PBNR (Prompt-based News Recommender) and RecPrompt from CIKM 2024, treats news recommendation as a text-to-text generation task.

The critical advantage for your use case is that the model can explain why something is relevant to you: "This article about the EU AI Act connects to your interest in AI governance and will matter for your work on the Esatto platform." That is impossible with pure similarity scoring.

The trade-off is latency and cost. LLM reasoning is expensive per call. In practice you use it as a re-ranking layer on top of a cheaper first-stage retrieval — retrieve the top 50 candidates with embeddings, then use the LLM to re-rank and annotate the top 10.

Personalized prompt construction Two-stage retrieve then rerank LLM-generated explanation per article

Personal knowledge graph modeling

Advanced

This approach builds a structured graph of entities and their relationships specifically for you. Every article you read contributes entities (people, companies, technologies, events) and the relationships between them to your personal knowledge graph. A novelty score for new articles is computed based on how many new entities or relationships it adds to your existing graph.

A 2019 patent from IBM (US10546028) formalizes this: filter articles based on how much genuinely new information they contain relative to your personal knowledge graph. This naturally solves a common problem — you stop seeing the fifth article about the same event because the graph already contains that knowledge.

Building a proper knowledge graph is technically involved. A practical starting point is using an LLM to extract entities and relationships from articles as you read them, storing them in a graph database like Neo4j, and using GraphRAG for relevance scoring against new articles.

Entity extraction via LLM Neo4j or similar graph DB Novelty scoring against known graph GraphRAG retrieval

Short-term and long-term interest separation

Modern

Research consistently shows that user interests operate at two distinct timescales. Your long-term interests are stable — software architecture, AI safety, Stockholm tech scene. Your short-term interests are transient — you are currently reading about NestJS because of the Lojalitetsprogram project, but that interest will fade after the presentation.

LSTUR (Microsoft, 2019) was an early influential model that fused long-term user embeddings with short-term sequential behavior. More recent work like the StageEvol model (2025) explicitly models stage-wise evolution: how your interests shift through distinct phases rather than continuously drifting.

The practical implementation: maintain two separate user profiles. The long-term profile updates slowly, weighted by recency decay. The short-term profile is a sliding window of your last 7 to 14 days. Blend them for ranking, with configurable weighting so you can boost recency when you are in a focused research sprint.

Dual user embedding Recency decay weighting Configurable blend ratio Session-aware context

Reinforcement learning from behavioral signals

Best for growth

The most powerful way to make the system improve over time is to treat your interaction behavior as a reward signal and use it to continuously update the ranking model. This is how large platforms like Google News and Onet operate at scale.

For a personal platform, a lightweight version works well: treat save, share, and long dwell time as strong positive rewards. Treat quick exit and scrolling past as weak negative signals. Use these rewards to update the weights in your user profile and ranking model, so articles similar to ones that generated strong rewards rank higher in future sessions.

The key insight from recent research is to use multiple behavioral signals rather than click-through rate alone, because CTR is noisy — you sometimes click things you end up not caring about. Dwell time weighted by personal reading speed is a much better signal of genuine interest.

Dwell time (personalized baseline) Save and share as strong reward Multi-armed bandit for exploration Iterative profile updates
03

Behavioral signals — what to capture

Not all signals are equally informative. The key insight from the research is that implicit signals need to be interpreted carefully — a quick click tells you very little, but a long dwell followed by a save tells you a lot.

Strong positive

  • Save / bookmark
  • Share to external tool
  • Full read (>90% scroll)
  • Return visit to same article
  • Copy excerpt or link

Moderate positive

  • Dwell time above personal baseline
  • Scroll past headline into body
  • Expand summary to full article
  • Tag or annotate

Ambiguous

  • Click-through (many false positives)
  • Short dwell (interrupted or rejected?)
  • Pause mid-scroll

Negative

  • Quick exit (<10s)
  • Explicit dismiss
  • Scroll past without pause
  • Hide source / topic

Research finding

A 2024 paper on dwell time injection (Arxiv 2405.12486) found that raw dwell time is unreliable as an absolute measure because people have different reading speeds and attention patterns. The better approach is to normalize dwell time against your own personal baseline — an article you read for twice your average dwell time is genuinely engaging, regardless of whether that baseline is 30 seconds or 3 minutes.

For your platform specifically

Because you are the only user, you can also use explicit lightweight signals that would be annoying at scale but work well in a personal tool: a quick "this connected to something I am working on" tag, a "I already knew this" dismiss, or a "teach me more about this" prompt. These are far more informative than any implicit signal and cost you only a second each.

User profile schema (conceptual)

// Stored in PostgreSQL + pgvector
user_profile {
  // Long-term interest embedding (updated weekly with decay)
  lt_embedding: vector(1536),

  // Short-term embedding (last 14 days, no decay)
  st_embedding: vector(1536),

  // Hierarchical interest tree
  interest_tree: {
    "software_architecture": { weight: 0.82, subs: ["clean_arch", "monorepo"] },
    "ai_ml": { weight: 0.91, subs: ["llm_infra", "agents", "safety"] },
    "swedish_tech": { weight: 0.65, subs: ["startups", "policy"] }
  },

  // Behavioral signal weights (personalized)
  signal_weights: {
    avg_dwell_seconds: 147,      // personal reading baseline
    dwell_threshold: 1.4,        // 1.4x baseline = strong positive
    save_reward: 1.0,
    full_read_reward: 0.6,
    quick_exit_penalty: -0.3
  },

  // Active contexts (boosts relevance while project is live)
  active_contexts: [
    { label: "Lojalitetsprogram", topics: ["loyalty_programs", "nestjs", "expo"] },
    { label: "Esatto outreach", topics: ["crm", "dotnet", "b2b_sales"] }
  ]
}

What to actually build — the recommended path

Given your use case — a professional personal platform, single user, goal of increasingly tailored intelligence — here is the recommended build sequence, ordered by highest return on investment.

Build sequence — phase by phase

Start lean, layer depth as you go

The most common mistake is trying to build the full system at once. The approach below gives you a genuinely useful system at each phase, so you get value immediately and can validate assumptions before investing in the harder parts.

01

RSS + embedding pipeline

Ingest feeds, embed articles, score against a manually curated interest embedding. Ship in a week.

02

Behavioral feedback loop

Capture dwell time and saves. Update user profile embedding. System starts learning from you.

03

Dual interest model

Split long-term and short-term profiles. Add active project contexts. Quality jumps noticeably.

04

LLM re-ranking + explanation

Add an LLM re-ranking pass on the top 30 candidates. Each article gets a "why this matters to you" note.

05

Knowledge graph

Extract entities from reads. Build novelty scoring. Dramatically reduces duplicate coverage.

The key insight for your platform

The LLM explanation layer is especially valuable for you because it transforms the news feed from a list of articles into an intelligence layer. Instead of "here are relevant articles," you get "this development in AI regulation connects to a gap you flagged in the Esatto platform architecture three weeks ago." That kind of bridging — between incoming news and your ongoing work context — is what separates a genuinely personal intelligence layer from a smarter RSS reader.

07

Cold start — how to handle it

The cold start problem is less severe for a personal platform than for a consumer app, but it still applies to new topic areas you have never read about, or after a major shift in your interests.

Explicit interest bootstrapping

On first run, prompt yourself to describe your interests in natural language. Use an LLM to convert that into a structured interest tree and an initial embedding. This gives you a usable profile from day one without any read history. Think of it as a one-time onboarding conversation: "I care about distributed systems, especially consistency and database internals. I follow the Swedish startup scene. I am currently building a loyalty platform on NestJS."

Popularity blending for new topics

When you enter a new topic area (say, a new project technology), you have no behavioral history for it yet. PP-Rec (2021) showed that blending personalized scores with time-decayed popularity scores for that domain is the most effective cold-start mitigation. Practically: when you add "Expo" to your active contexts, initially surface the most-engaged Expo articles from the past 30 days, then let your own behavioral signals gradually override the popularity signal.

Context injection from ongoing work

Your active projects and current learning goals are an underused source of context. Instead of waiting for behavioral signals to develop for a new area, directly inject your current project contexts into the ranking prompt. "I am building a vending machine loyalty platform for my thesis. Prioritize articles about behavioral economics of loyalty programs and mobile payment UX." This gives the LLM layer immediate context without requiring any read history.

08

Pitfalls to design around

Most personalization systems fail in predictable ways. Knowing these in advance lets you design defenses into the architecture from the start.

Filter bubble reinforcement

A pure personalization system converges to showing you only what you already know you like, which eliminates serendipity and gradually impoverishes the feed. The fix is deliberate diversity injection: reserve 15 to 20 percent of feed slots for articles from your interest neighborhood but slightly outside your established reading patterns. Research calls this the "explore vs exploit" trade-off.

Click-through rate bias

Clickbait and sensational headlines get high CTR even when the underlying content is low quality. If you optimize primarily on click signals, the feed degrades over time. Use full-read rate and save rate as the primary quality signals, and treat CTR as a weak soft signal only.

Recency bias

Without a timeliness component, an embedding-based system will surface older highly-relevant articles repeatedly. Add a time-decay term: score = relevance × freshness_decay(age), where freshness decays more slowly for long-form analysis pieces than for breaking news.

Interest drift without forgetting

Your interests evolve. If old reads are weighted equally to recent ones, the profile becomes a museum of past interests rather than a living representation of current ones. Use exponential recency decay on the long-term profile update, so a read from two years ago contributes much less to your current profile than a read from last week.
